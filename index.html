Scope

The workshop takes a broad view of the “virtual cell” as an emerging grand challenge for modern computational biology.

The term “virtual cell” is sometimes used narrowly to describe models that predict how cells respond to gene knockouts or drug perturbations. Here, we use it more broadly: as an evolving effort to build increasingly accurate computational representations of cellular systems, spanning genes, proteins, regulatory networks, and higher-order biological processes.

Realising this vision will require advances not only in model development, but also in how progress is measured. Rigorous benchmarking, evaluation frameworks, and shared datasets are essential for understanding the capabilities and limitations of biological foundation models, comparing approaches across modalities, and identifying meaningful milestones toward increasingly predictive and mechanistic representations of the cell.

By emphasizing both modeling and evaluation, the workshop aims to develop a clearer picture of where the field stands today and what advances are needed to move closer to a practical virtual cell.

Biography

Eli Cole is a computer scientist whose primary interests are machine learning and computer vision, with an emphasis on building machine learning systems that enable scientific discovery. He is currently a Senior Research Scientist and Team Lead at GenBio AI, where he works on large multimodal AI models for biology, particularly at the cell and tissue level.

Previously, Eli was a Research Scientist at Altos Labs in Peter Kharchenko’s group, where he worked closely with experimental scientists to develop machine learning systems for discovering biologically significant cell and tissue states from data such as scRNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics, histology, IF, and IHC. He earned his Ph.D. at Caltech under Pietro Perona, focusing on computer vision, self-supervision, and spatial representation learning, and spent part of his Ph.D. working with Serge Belongie at Google Research. He also completed internships at Microsoft Research and the U.S. Air Force Research Laboratory. He received his B.S.E. from Duke University with a double major in electrical engineering and mathematics.

Biography

Henrik Nielsen is an Associate Professor in Bioinformatics at DTU Health Tech and leads the Protein Sorting group. His research focuses on computational modelling of biological sequences and on understanding how biologically meaningful signals can be learned directly from sequence data. He has worked with neural networks for sequence modelling since the 1990s and is one of the original developers of SignalP, a widely used method for predicting signal peptides. Invited speaker

Emily Jin

Research Scientist · AI & Robotics Institute, Ellison Institute of Technology

“Monera: Learning Bacterial Biology at Full-Genome Scale”

Abstract

Foundation models can learn rich representations of genomic sequences, but many bacterial phenotypes of interest emerge from interactions distributed across the entire genome. In this talk, I will introduce Monera, a new approach that combines genomic foundation modelling with optimal transport to generate meaningful embeddings for complete prokaryotic genomes. This will also include a discussion on the challenges of sequence representation, genome-scale context, and building meaningful evaluations.

Biography

Emily is a Research Scientist in the AI & Robotics Institute at the Ellison Institute of Technology. She recently completed her Ph.D. in Computer Science at the University of Oxford under the supervision of Michael Bronstein and İsmail Ceylan, and in collaboration with the Quantitative Biology team at AstraZeneca. Her research focuses on developing machine learning methods to help address challenges in drug discovery, such as target identification, molecular property prediction, molecular crystal structure prediction, and DNA foundation modelling.

Relevant work at Altos Labs

Researchers at Altos Labs have recently focused on both modelling and evaluating cellular responses to perturbations. PerturBench provides a standardized framework for benchmarking single-cell perturbation-response models across diverse datasets and evaluation metrics, including rank-based measures designed to expose important model failures such as mode collapse.

More recently, the team developed PRiMeFlow, an end-to-end flow-matching generative model for predicting responses to genetic and small-molecule perturbations directly in gene-expression space. The model was developed and evaluated within PerturBench, and its architecture formed the basis of the Generalist Prize-winning entry in the first ARC Virtual Cell Challenge. This line of work sits directly at the intersection of virtual-cell modelling, model design, perturbation response, and biologically meaningful evaluation. Biography

Fabian Theis is a pioneer in biomedical artificial intelligence and machine learning. His research develops scalable statistical learning frameworks and biomedical foundation models for the integrative analysis of single-cell, spatial, imaging, and clinical data. A central focus of his work is multimodal data integration across biological scales, with the aim of enabling predictive and data-driven precision medicine. His lab develops widely used computational methods and open-source software, including tools in the Scanpy and scverse ecosystem.

Fabian is Head of the Computational Health Center at Helmholtz Munich and Director of the Institute of Computational Biology. He is also Chair for Mathematical Models of Biological Systems at the Technical University of Munich and Scientific Director of the Helmholtz Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Unit (Helmholtz.AI).
